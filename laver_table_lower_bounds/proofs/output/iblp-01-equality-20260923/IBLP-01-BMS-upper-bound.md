## 1. Why simple line-of-sight projection is not enough

When a q interface row is copied, it moves a series of ports before the critical point to the end of the row, rather than simply translating the addresses "after the critical point."

Therefore, "let q be directly equal to the BMS line number, and then regard the tangent point as the parent item of that line" does not satisfy the nested parent chains in the ordinary BMS. It is known that the lower segment S=A[2] will expose this problem.

This document does not require the parent table to be equal. The target BMS can have additional columns and additional ancestors. The key supplement is the **Bridge relation** that is generated by the entire interface chain. Bridge relationships can constrain the rows before the interface chain, as well as the rows after the interface chain; however, they do not constrain the current last row. This last point allows for the local preparation of the target to proceed.

## 3. covers invariant properties: fundamental edges plus bridge edges

Let M be the ordinary standard BMS. Its columns are numbered starting from 0. Denote x≺_r y as indicating that x is a non-empty parent chain ancestor of y in the rth row. The ancestors of a BMS's higher rows are also ancestors of the lower rows.

Provide a strictly increasing address mapping

$$
e:\{0,...,N\}\longrightarrow\{0,...,|M|-1\},
\quad e(0)=0,\quad e(N)=|M|-1.
$$

The coverage requirements are as follows.

### 3.1 Basic Relations

- When q_i=0, it is required that e(P(i))≺_0 and e(i).
- When q_i=k>0, it is required that

$$
e(p_i)\prec_k e(i),\qquad
e(v_t)\prec_t e(i)\quad(0\le t<k),\tag{3.1}
$$

Among them, V_i=(v_0,...,v_k).

This covers all the P parent relationships: when q_i>0, it takes the t=k-1 value, and then drops to the 0 row.

### 3.2 Bridge Relations

Take any interface chain W=(w_0,...,w_h) in the pattern, and take a value from any row that is U or V. If

$$
1\le s<t\le h,\quad
q_i=s,\quad p_i=w_s,\quad
w_t\text{ is }i\text{ is a strict P-ancestor},\quad i<N,
$$

Then an additional requirement

$$
\boxed{e(w_t)\prec_s e(i).}\tag{3.2}
$$

Note: All numbering symbols should be retained as is.

- The source line of the interface chain does not have to precede i, nor does it have to follow i.
- Only i<N is required to have bridges. The last line still satisfies all fundamental relationships, but there are no bridge requirements.
- Do not add or modify the IBLP edges; this is an additional condition for the target coverage certificate.
- The required colors never exceed max_i q_i; the 0 row is also used for the zero interface row.

### Every finite pattern has a standard coverage for 3.3

Every row of it is a complete ancestral chain, thus e(i)=i covers all fundamental edges and bridge edges. This is a standard BMS format, and it is strictly less than B_(H+2).

## The 7. bridge relation remains intact in a complete copy.

This is the core of upper bounds, and cannot be overlooked from the concept of "parent edge preservation."

Let k>0 be defined. The new bridge originates from a chain W'=(w'_0,...,w'_h), a non-terminal i', and 1≤s<t≤h, satisfying

$$
q_{i'}=s,\quad p_{i'}=w'_s,\quad
w'_t\prec_P i'.\tag{7.1}
$$

Chains can originate from old prefixes or from the replication zone; i' can also be in two positions. The following four cases cover all possibilities for bridges.

### 7.1 old chain, old row

Prefix remains unchanged. The original bridge has been covered.

### 7.2 Old chain, copied row

Write i'=i+d, c≤i<N. The new critical point p_(i') is in the old prefix, therefore it can only be:

**Fixed tangent point.** p_i=w_s<a. If w_t≥c, the old chain extends from w_s<a to w_t≥c, which will place a within the interface chain of some c, violating the source barrier. Therefore, w_t<c. The old prefix P of the new line is also an ancestor of c before c, so there originally exists w_t≺_P i. The original bridge w_t→i is retained by the formula maintained by the old prefix ancestor.

**Moving the critical point.** is composed of (2.2),p_i=u_s,w_s=v_s,s<k. w_t is an ancestor of P in the old and new replication zones of the old prefix, therefore it is part of the ancestor chain of the old N. Use the outer U provided in i>c to create a bridge in the c→i line numbered s. At time i=c, no further connections are required. Thus, we obtain w_t→i'.

### 7.3 Copy chain, old row

Write W'=f(W), W comes from some original line j≥c. Because of w'_t<i'<N, there must be w_t<c.

**w_t<a.** w_s, w_t are both fixed. The original chain W and the old row i' have generated the required bridges, regardless of whether j is before or after i'. Therefore, prefix coverage is sufficient. This is precisely why the origin of bridges cannot be confined to "future rows".

**w_t=u_l.** is derived from (2.3), with δ=l-t≥0, then w_s=u_(s+δ), hence the cut point of the old line is v_(s+δ). From (2.4), δ=0. Therefore, w'_s=v_s, w'_t=v_t. The image interface chain of the original line, V, has already generated exactly the same bridges for this old line; the coverage of the old prefix remains unchanged.

w_t=a is impossible, because t≥1, and a cannot be an internal point of the replication interface chain.

### 7.4 replication chain, replication row

Write W'=f(W), i'=i+d, c≤i<N. f is a bijection, so p_i=w_s.

**w_t≥c.** All related P ancestral relationships are within the replicated subtree. After pulling back, they become the original bridge w_t→i. The block's ancestral relationship is maintained to create a new bridge.

**w_t<a.** w_s is also fixed. Prefix ancestors that are below a are still ancestors of the original critical point c, hence the original bridge w_t→i already exists. The old prefix and ancestor are retained to give the new bridge.

**w_t=u_l<c.** is derived from (2.3),w_s=u_(s+δ),δ=l-t≥0. Also, since q_i=s,p_i=w_s, (2.2) forces δ=0. Therefore, the new chain's two points are v_s,v_t, and s<t<k. The v_t of the old last column's relation in the t row is retained as the s row relation in the new seam. If i>c, then along the outer U, the c→i row bridge in the s row enters the replication row; if i=c, then the new seam itself is the required row.

The last line of the new work does not have bridge requirements, so i=N does not need to be dealt with in this section. All of its fundamental relations have been handled in the 6 section.

## 9. Why is this the order-preserving upper bound for the entire standard domain?

Just obtaining the expanded fundamental rank is not enough; it is also necessary to verify the original comparison and reachability relationships.

### 9.1 strictly reduces the original comparison key with each non-zero actual expansion.

The tail is strictly reduced.

When the positive interface is controlled by k>0, the first change occurs in the old last row N. The seam q_c belongs to {0,1,k}.

- q_c=k: The image interface of the source critical point row is exactly the outer U.
Its own tangent point is strictly less than a; otherwise, a would be the internal reference for that row. The interface part of the new key is the same as the old V, and the last row head point is strictly less than the old a.
- q_c=0: The new key is the first two elements of the old key, strictly a prefix.
- q_c=1<k: The new key is (N,P(N),a').
If the source cut point is fixed before a, then it is a'<a≤P²(N); if the source cut point is u_1, then it is a'=v_0≤v_(k-2)=P²(N). Thus, the new key is either smaller than the third item, or it is a true prefix of the old key.

When there is no interface control and no successor, the old keys are (N,c)); the second item of the new keys is P(c)<c. Regardless of the number of interfaces on the critical point row itself, the level is strictly reduced.

The above comparison is irrelevant to the subsequent replication blocks, as the initial difference has been fixed. Due to the absence of marked completions, it applies to all strictly standard descendants.

The root A is also closed by fundamental terms: A[0] is a true prefix; when n≥1, the A[n]'s 4th row key is (4,3,0), and the A's 4th row key is (4,3,2). Therefore, these fundamental terms are indeed strictly less than A.

### The prefix 9.2 in the fundamental sequence provides a finite descending comparability of the standard domain.

In practice, [0] exactly deletes the last row; non-zero expansions do not change the original prefix. For each member of [D], the fundamental terms are arranged in a prefix chain according to their order (allowing [1]=[2]-like pauses). The fundamental terms of [A] are also formed into prefix chains by proven closed formulas.

Therefore, any two finite reachable expressions of the same root can be compared in finite descent. The proof can be generalized to the total length of two histories: at the first bifurcation, the smaller fundamental term is a prefix of the larger fundamental term. Continuing down from the larger fundamental term, either retain this prefix or must exactly reach it; it cannot be skipped, as deleting the tail would reduce the width, and only one row is reduced at a time. The latter case can be generalized from the common prefix, while the former case can be directly truncated for comparison.

In combination with 9.1, the standard domain's original comparison is exactly consistent with the finite descending order; different standard forms cannot also produce the same keys but different orders solely through symbols like the asterisk.

### 9.3 combines all parts below A_n

Every strict descendant of A is below some A_n, and therefore belongs to D. The same global definition E applies to all of these cases, eliminating the need to manually concatenate the encodings of different n.

If X<Y<A, then by 9.2, Y, it can be finitely non-emptyly expanded into X; and by (8.2), E(X)<E(Y)), it follows that E is a strictly ordered mapping from the entire strict standard lower segment to the ordinary standard BMS.

This proves the opposite of an upper bound. Combined with the existing embedding from the standard domain of ordinary BMSs to this subsection, both sides are well-ordered, and the two inequalities of the order type merge into an equal sign.

A itself continues to expand into some A_n, therefore the entire expansion of A is also well-founded. The conclusion of "no completion" still starts from A_n; A indeed uses completion when it generates fundamental terms.

The earlier exploration batches stopped at a budget of 22 seconds, completing 2,045 actual simulations; another larger sparse host batch stopped at 23 seconds. These batches were bounded error-checking, not full-depth exhaustive.

Every program has a time limit, a maximum width, and a maximum number of preparation steps. The memory capacity is far lower than the user's 32GB limits. The budget exception inherits from BaseException to avoid being swallowed by the fault-tolerant branches of the original implementation. There is no unbounded fixed metric descent search, nor is the completion function of the original implementation turned off.

The general conclusions are derived from the coverage construction, four types of bridge closures, and minimal coverage proofs in section 2–9. They do not come from the aforementioned test numbers. This is a paper proof, not a Lean certificate.
