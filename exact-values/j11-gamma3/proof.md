# $J_{11}(\gamma_3)=\gamma_{2^{2048}}$ · [中文版](proof.zh-CN.md)

Fix an I3 witness, a nontrivial elementary embedding $j:V_\lambda\to V_\lambda$. Take the standard height $\lambda=\sup_{i<\omega}\kappa_i$, where $\kappa_0=\operatorname{cr}j,\ \kappa_{i+1}=j(\kappa_i)$. Let $\gamma_0,\gamma_1,\ldots$ enumerate in increasing order the critical points of the algebra generated by $j$ under embedding application. Put $J_1=j$ and $J_{a+1}=J_a(j)$. If $J_z(\gamma_t)=\gamma_{f_z(t)}$, the natural number we seek is $f_{11}(3)$.

**Conclusion.** In the given I3 realization,

$$
  \boxed{f_{11}(3)=256^{256}=2^{2048}.}
$$

Appendices A–E derive the two lemmas of Section 1 from the critical spectrum of I3 and the finite-table rules. Sections 2 and 3 establish a period estimate and compare two composite rows. Starting from the eight entries computed in Appendix F, Section 7 applies the local arguments of Sections 4 and 5 at each order to prove the equality. This route uses only the second-level bit-stretching theorem. If one invokes Drápal's third-level diagonal homomorphism directly, Sections 1–6 give a shorter proof. [Second-level bit-stretching theorem](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-4049(94)00142-1) · [Abstract on the third level, p. 4](https://www.cs.du.edu/~petr/milehigh/2017/old/booklet.pdf#page=4)

## 1. The old table, bit stretching and two lemmas

Let $A_u$ be the ordinary binary Laver table with $2^u$ elements. Use the representatives $0,\ldots,2^u-1$, so zero ends a row's period. The successor and row recursion are $x*_u1=x+1\bmod 2^u$ and $x*_u(y+1)=(x*_u y)*_u(x+1)$. Write $[e]_u$ for the representative of an embedding $e$ in the table. The period exponent of row $z$ is

$$
 p_u(z)=\min\{t:f_z(t)\ge u\}.
$$

The application successor gives $[J_z]_u=z\bmod 2^u$. Use $f_C(t)$ also for an embedding or composite $C$. If two embeddings have the same representative in $A_u$, their image indices agree after truncation at $u$. Ordinary function composition has finite-table formula $[C\circ J_i]_u=[C]_u*_u(i+1)-1\bmod 2^u$. The nonzero entries in the first period of a row are strictly increasing. On passing to the next order the period stays the same or doubles. The upper-half copy retains the old period. [Dougherty II, Section 2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=4)

Fix the parameters

$$
 N=256,\quad T=N^N,\quad d=T+1,\quad a=2^N-2,\quad g=a-1.
$$

For $y=\sum_i y_i2^i\in A_N$ define the map

$$
 P(y)=\sum_{i=0}^{N-1} y_i(2^{N^{i+1}}-2^{N^i}).
$$

For the short proof, take Drápal's third-level diagonal parameters $H_3(0)=2,\ H_3(i+1)=H_3(i)^{H_3(i)}$. Then $H_3(2)=256,\ H_3(3)=T$, giving the homomorphism $P:A_N\to A_T$. Since $g*_T1=P(1)=a$, row recursion gives $g*_T y=P(y)$ at every argument. Its period is $2^N$. Substituting $y=2^t$ yields

$$
 f_g(t)=N^t\ (0\le t<N), \qquad f_g(N)\ge T. \tag{1}
$$

Appendices E and B–D prove the following two lemmas, respectively.

* For $1\le i<a$ we have $f_i(N)\ge T$. More generally, this holds for every row whose low $N$ bits lie in that range. If the bit interval $[N,T]$ contains at least one 1, then $f_i(N)>T$. Consequently a nonzero representative $x$ in $A_d$ divisible by $2^N$ satisfies $x*_d y=x+y\ (1\le y\le a)$. Throughout, $x+y<2^d$.
* Put $Q_0=J_{11}(J_3),\,G=Q_0(j)$. The representative of $G$ in $A_d$ is $g$. Using $f_{11}(0)=2,\,f_{11}(1)=4,\,f_{11}(2)=256$ and $f_3(0)=2,\,f_3(1)=3$, covariance gives $Q_0(\gamma_2)=\gamma_{256}$ and $Q_0(\gamma_4)=J_{11}(\gamma_3)$. Also $j(\gamma_2)=\gamma_4$, so $G(\gamma_{256})=Q_0(\gamma_4)=J_{11}(\gamma_3)$. Once we prove $f_g(N)=T$, the actual output can be read at $T<d$.

Let $h_w$ repeat each binary digit $w$ times and put $\ell_w=2^w-2$ and $e_w=2^w-1$. The second-level bit-stretching theorem says that for $w=2^b$ and $b>0$ the map $h_w:A_k\to A_{kw}$ is a homomorphism whenever $k\le 2^{\,2^{\nu_2(b)+1}}$. In the target table, row $\ell_w$ equals $h_w$, since the two agree at 1 and satisfy the same row recursion. Here $w=N^m$, so $b=8m$, and the permitted source orders extend at least to $65536>N+1$. In particular,

$$
 f_{\ell_w}(N)=Nw. \tag{2}
$$

To obtain equality, read the 2-adic valuation of $h_w(2^N)$ at source order $N+1$. [Drápal 1995](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-4049(94)00142-1) · [Parameters and the row formula](notes/c3-gamma1024-drapal.md)

## 2. A period bound for every carry width

For each $m\ge1$ put $w=N^m$. Then

$$
 1\le x\le2^w-2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
 p_{Nw}(x)\le w,\qquad f_x(w)\ge Nw . \tag{3}
$$

Induct on $m$. Take $b$ to be the largest power of two at most $m$ and put $v=N^b$. Then $v=2^{2^j}$ for some $j$, with $v\mid w$ and $H=Nw\le v^2$. Write $x$ as a string of $v$-bit words of total length $w$.

Suppose one word is neither zero nor all 1s. Let its starting bit be $s$. Temporarily set all $s$ lower bits to 1. Since $v\mid s$, Dougherty's Theorem 7.2(b) bounds the index of image $v$ of the filled row from below by $s+v^2\ge H$. Its period exponent in $A_H$ is therefore at most $v$. Write the nonzero place values in the binary expansion of $s$ as $b_1<\cdots<b_k$, in ascending order. Successively restore the $b_j$ bits at the upper end of the remaining filled region. Each block starts at a multiple of $b_j$. Before step $j$ the period exponent is at most $v+\sum_{h<j}b_h\le b_j$, since each $b_h$ is a multiple of $v$.

Block $j$ starts at $z=\sum_{i>j}b_i$, so $b_j\mid z$. In reverse notation the forward row $x'$ corresponds to $2^H-x'=(2^H-1-x')+1$. Before restoration write this as $X+1$, where $2^{z+b_j}\mid X$ because the portion still to restore is all 1s in forward notation. After restoration it is $X+2^z y+1$, with $y$ the bitwise complement of the restored block. For $y=2^{b_j}-1$, Corollary 4.2 increases the period exponent by $b_j$. For $0<y<2^{b_j}-1$, Lemma 4.6 bounds the exponent by $b_j$. For $y=0$ the row is unchanged. The reverse row has the same period as in $A_H$, so the next exponent is at most $v+\sum_{i\le j}b_i$. The initial estimate $v+\sum_{i<j}b_i\le b_j$ follows since distinct powers of two $b_i/v<b_j/v$ sum to at most $b_j/v-1$. The final exponent is at most $s+v\le w$. For $s=0$ the initial estimate already suffices. [Dougherty, Theorem 4.1, Corollary 4.2 and Lemma 4.6](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=12) · [Theorem 7.2(b)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=21)

If every word is zero or all 1s, then $x=h_v(u)$ with $1\le u\le 2^{w/v}-2$. The interval is empty when $m=b$. Otherwise put $t=m-b<m$. The induction hypothesis gives $p=p_{H/v}(u)\le w/v<H/v$. Since $H/v\le v$ and the largest source order allowed for $v=2^{2^j}$ is $v^2$, we may use $h_v:A_{H/v}\to A_H$. Transport $u*2^p=0$ along this homomorphism. Row $x$ returns to zero at argument $h_v(2^p)$, whose 2-adic valuation is exactly $vp$. Thus $p_H(x)\le vp\le w$, completing the induction.

## 3. Two composites in the first period

In the old table $A_T$, suppose $i+1<j+1$ lie inside the first period of an actual embedding $D$ and that both outputs exceed 1. Write $Z_i,Z_j$ for the old-table residues of $D\circ J_i,D\circ J_j$, in that order. If

$$
 f_{Z_i}(r)=T,\qquad
 T<U_j=f_D(f_j(t))\le U_i=f_D(f_i(r)),
$$

then $f_{Z_j}(t)=T$.

Proof. The native row $J_{Z_i}$ has period exponent $r$ at $A_T$, rising to $r+1$ at $A_{T+1}$. The actual composition has the same exponent at $A_T$ and its image of index $r$ is $U_i$. Its period exponent therefore stays at $r$ through height $T,\ldots,U_i$. Suppose a zero occurs in the bits starting at $T,\ldots,U_i-1$ and take the first such bit $v$. In general, filling $0<Z<2^T$ with 1 in bit $T,\ldots,v-1$ and 0 in bit $v$ gives reverse row number $(2^T-Z)+2^v$ at $A_{v+1}$. The native row has reverse number $(2^T-Z)+2^T$ at $A_{T+1}$. If $v=T$ the numbers coincide. If $v>T$, then $0<2^T-Z<2^T<2^v$, so Biane's Proposition 5.1 gives the same period for the two reverse rows. Taking $Z=Z_i$ would force the actual composition to have exponent $r+1$ already at $v+1\le U_i$, a contradiction. Thus every bit in the segment equals 1. [Biane, Proposition 5.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.00548#page=10)

In $A_{U_j}$ the earlier output $D*(i+1)$ lies in the final interval of length $2^T$. Projection preserves multiplication, so the higher-order period is a multiple of the old one. Both inputs still lie inside the same period. Hence the later output $D*(j+1)$ also lies in that interval. Both old residues exceed 1, so subtracting 1 preserves the upper bits. The representative of the actual composite $D\circ J_j$ in $A_{U_j}$ is therefore exactly $2^{U_j}-2^T+Z_j$. Its period exponent is $t$ at $U_j$ and $t+1$ at $U_j+1$, since the image of index $t$ is exactly $U_j$. Every lift from $T$ through $U_j$ takes the upper-half copy and preserves the period, giving $p_T(Z_j)=t$. The doubling at $U_j$ must take the lower copy. Use Biane's Proposition 5.1 again with reverse low part $2^T-Z_j>0$. This gives $p_{T+1}(Z_j)=t+1$ and hence $f_{Z_j}(t)=T$.

We shall also need a one-step fact. If an old residue $Z$ satisfies $f_Z(q)>T$, then every embedding $C$ with this old residue satisfies $f_C(q)>T$. In $A_d$ the period of the low copy divides $2^q$. The high copy keeps its old period, which also divides $2^q$.

## 4. A carry with arbitrary upper bits

Let $1\le r<2^{128}-1$ be odd and write

$$
 m=\nu_2(r+1),\quad u=r-(2^m-1),\quad w=N^m,\quad D=P(u),\quad A=P(r).
$$

Then $2^{m+1}\mid u,\ 2^{Nw}\mid D$ and

$$
 A=D+\ell_w,\qquad P(r+1)=D+h_w(a).
$$

We shall prove $A*_d a=P(r+1)$, including the case where bit $Nw$ of $D$ is already 1. Here $1\le m\le127$ and $Nw<T$.

First establish the following prefix in the old table.

$$
 A*_T y=D+h_w(y)\qquad(1\le y\le a). \tag{4}
$$

The $P$ homomorphism gives its endpoint because $a=P(1)$. After projection to $A_{Nw}$, row $A$ is the full row of $h_w$ on $A_N$. Thus the old period has length at least $2^N$. Its first $a$ terms lie in the first period between $A+1>D$ and $D+h_w(a)<D+2^{Nw}$, and their low $Nw$ bits are determined by projection. This proves (4).

For $1\le i<a$ put $Z_i=D+h_w(i+1)-1$. By (4) this represents $J_A\circ J_i$ in $A_T$. We next establish

$$
 f_{Z_i}(N)>T\qquad(1\le i<a). \tag{5}
$$

First, $f_r(N-2)\ge N$. If the odd number satisfies $r>1$, at least two of its low $N$ bits are 1, and the image of $J_r$ avoids the indices of all 1 bits. Thus $0,\ldots,N-1$ can hold at most $N-2$ images. In the case $r=1$, use $f_1(2)=4$ and strict increase. [Dougherty, Lemma 2.9 and the low-index values](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204)

Also $A=P(r)$ is the old-table representative of the embedding application $J_g(J_r)$. By covariance and (1),

$$
 f_A(N^{N-2})\ge T.
$$

The final outer composite $Z_g$ corresponds to $i=g=a-1$. Since $f_g(N-2)=N^{N-2}$, we have $f_{Z_g}(N-2)\ge T$ and then $f_{Z_g}(N-1)>T$. For every $i<g$, Section 1 gives $f_i(N)\ge T>N^{N-1}$, so the two endpoints of the actual composites satisfy

$$
 f_A(f_i(N))>f_A(f_g(N-1))>T.
$$

Hence $f_{Z_i}(N)\ge T$. In the case of equality, applying Section 3 to $i<g$ at indices $N,N-1$ would give $f_{Z_g}(N-1)=T$, a contradiction. The case $i=g$ follows directly from the strict bound at the lower index. This proves (5).

## 5. Lifting the prefix one entry at a time to $A_{T+1}$

Fix $1\le i<a$ and put $W=D+h_w(i)$ and $E=2^w-1$. In the old table,

$$
 W*_T E=D+h_w(i+1). \tag{6}
$$

We need the row period here. Write $l=\nu_2(i+1)$, $i=z+2^l-1$, $p=lw$ and $P_0=D+h_w(z)$. Then $2^{p+w}\mid P_0$ and

$$
 W=P_0+2^p-1,\qquad D+h_w(i+1)=P_0+2^p E.
$$

From $i+1\le 2^N-2$ we obtain $l\le N-1$, hence $p+w\le Nw$. In $A_{p+w}$, row $W$ equals row $2^p-1$. If $p>0$, then by $w\mid p$ and Lemma 6.2 the index of its image number $w-1$ is $p+w-1$. If $p=0$, use the identity row directly. Thus the old period is at least $2^w$. The positive residue of $A+1=D+E$ modulo that period is at least $E$, since all its low $w$ bits equal 1. Strict increase along the first period and (4) give

$$
 D<W*_T E\le W*_T(A+1)=D+h_w(i+1)<D+2^{Nw}.
$$

In $A_{Nw}$ the left-hand product projects to $h_w(i)*h_w(1)=h_w(i+1)$, giving (6). This also covers the case where $D$ has consecutive 1s starting at bit $Nw$. [Dougherty, Lemma 6.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=19)

By (6), $Z_i$ is also the old residue of the ordinary composite $J_W\circ J_{\ell_w}$. By (5) and the one-step fact of Section 3, image $N$ of this actual composite has index strictly above $T$. Equation (2) gives

$$
 \boxed{f_W(Nw)>T,\qquad p_d(W)\le Nw.} \tag{7}
$$

Equation (7) follows from the two descriptions of the old residue, independently of the new-table product to be proved. Hence in row $W$ of the new table the right arguments $D+E$ and $E$ have the same residue.

We now check every intermediate image needed for Lemma 6.7. In the old table, row $W$ increases strictly from entry 1, which equals $P_0+2^p$, through entry $E$, which equals $P_0+2^p E$. Every value is divisible by $2^p$, by projection to $A_p$. For $p=0$ this holds automatically. Hence

$$
 W*_T v=P_0+2^p v\qquad(1\le v\le E).
$$

Let $C_v=P_0+2^p v-1$ be the remainder of $J_W\circ J_{v-1}$. For $2\le v\le E$, from (3) we get $f_{v-1}(w)\ge Nw$, and (7) gives $f_W(Nw)>T$. Thus, $f_{C_v}(w)\ge T$. If $v<E$ and it takes equal amounts, the 3 section is transported from $v-1$ to $E-1=\ell_w$ in the $W$ row, with the input indices before and after being $w,N$. The actual image subscript is precisely $f_W(Nw)\le f_W(f_{v-1}(w))$. Therefore, $f_{C_E}(N)=T$, but $C_E=Z_i$ contradicts (5). $v=E$ is directly given by (5) and $w\ge N$. It can be seen that

$$
 f_{P_0+2^p v-1}(w)>T\qquad(2\le v\le E). \tag{8}
$$

Apply Dougherty's Lemma 6.7 to the background embedding $J_{P_0}$ with width $w$, position $l$ and argument $E$. Use the identity embedding if $P_0=0$. Its critical point is at least $\gamma_{p+w}$, $w$ is a power of two and $2\le E\le2^w$. The required cutoff is the minimum of the images in (8), hence at least $\gamma_{T+1}$. In the target table,

$$
 W*_d E=P_0+2^p E=D+h_w(i+1).
$$

By (7) we may replace $E$ by $A+1=D+E$. Applying the defining recursion starting at $A*_d1=D+e_w$ gives

$$
 A*_d y=D+h_w(y)\qquad(1\le y\le a),\qquad
 P(r)*_d a=P(r+1). \tag{9}
$$

The two inductions concern the argument $i$ within a row and the outer parameter $r$ in the next section. Equations (5)–(8) already hold before the outer induction. [Dougherty, Lemma 6.7, including the identity background](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=19)

## 6. The midpoint determines the original endpoint

If $0<r<2^{128}-1$ is even, the additive prefix in Section 1 and $2^N\mid P(r)$ give $P(r)*_d a=P(r)+a=P(r+1)$. For odd inputs use (9). Recursing from $g*_d1=a$ gives

$$
 g*_d(2^{128}-1)=P(2^{128}-1)=2^{W_0}-2,\qquad W_0=N^{128}=2^{1024}.
$$

Let $E_{128}=J_{2^{128}-1}$. Dougherty's lemma 6.2 takes a displacement $128=2^7$, and gives $f_{E_{128}}(i)=128+i$ that holds for $0\le i<256$. In particular, $E_{128}(\gamma_{128})=\gamma_{256}$. Therefore, $J_g(E_{128})$ sends $\gamma_{W_0}=J_g(\gamma_{128})$ to $J_g(\gamma_{256})$. The target table that was just determined is $J_{2^{W_0}-2}$. Since $W_0=2^{2^{10}}$, Dougherty's theorem 7.2(c) states that the critical sequence of this primal line begins at $\gamma_1\mapsto\gamma_{W_0}\mapsto\gamma_{W_0^2}$. And $W_0^2=T<d$, so an exact output can be read, resulting in $f_g(N)=T$. From the normal form of the 1 section, we obtain $J_{11}(\gamma_3)=\gamma_T$. [Dougherty, Lemma 6.2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=19) · [Theorem 7.2(c)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=21)

Equivalently, the entries in the ordinary finite table satisfy

$$
 11*_{T+1}8=2^T,\qquad
 (2^{16777216}-2)*_{T+1}2^{65536}=2^T.
$$

The first equality follows because the 2-adic valuation of $11*_{T+1}2^3$ is exactly $T$. For the second equality take $g*_d7=P(7)=2^{N^3}-2$ within the same verified prefix. Dougherty computed $J_7(\gamma_2)=\gamma_N$, so $J_g(J_7)$ sends $\gamma_{N^2}=J_g(\gamma_2)$ to $J_g(\gamma_N)=\gamma_T$. Since $T<d$, the product of row $2^{N^3}-2$ with $2^{N^2}$ has 2-adic valuation exactly $T$. [Dougherty II, p. 25](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=25)

## 7. Lifting from lower orders without the third-level homomorphism

For a table order $t$ and a nonnegative integer $y=\sum_j y_j2^j$ put

$$
 B_t(y)=\left(\sum_{j\ge0} y_j(2^{N^{j+1}}-2^{N^j})\right)\bmod 2^t.
$$

We shall prove directly that

$$
 g*_t y=B_t(y)\quad\text{for every }N^3+1\le t\le T+1,\ y\ge0. \tag{10}
$$

**Starting order.** Appendix F computes the first eight entries of row $g$ in $A_{N^3+1}$. The first seven are $B_{N^3+1}(1),\ldots,B_{N^3+1}(7)$ and the eighth is $2^{N^3}$. The fourth has 2-adic valuation $N^2$ and the eighth has 2-adic valuation $N^3$, so $f_g(2)=N^2,\ f_g(3)=N^3$. The period in $A_{N^3}$ is 8 and doubles to 16 at the starting order. Each old nonzero value occurs twice, first in its low copy and then its high copy. The remaining entries therefore also satisfy (10).

The last two zeros in **An independent lower bound on the exponent.** $g$ are $1,N$, so $f_g(0)=1,\ f_g(1)=N$. For $r\ge1$, take $E_r=J_{2^r-1}$, and its first two images have subscripts $r,r+1$. Embed $J_g(E_r)$ with $\gamma_0$ fixed, send $\gamma_1$ to $\gamma_{f_g(r)}$, and send $\gamma_N$ to $\gamma_{f_g(r+1)}$. The linear bound from Dougherty's lemma 7.1 gives $f_g(r+1)\ge N f_g(r)$. Therefore, for each $h$, $f_g(h)\ge N^h$. [Dougherty, Lemma 7.1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/math/9503204#page=20)

It is known that **Range of the induction step.**, and 10) holds in $t$, and also $N^3+1\le t\le T$. Take the unique $h$ to make $N^{h-1}<t\le N^h$, hence $4\le h\le N$. The old row period index is $h$, and all $j<h$ have $f_g(j)=N^j$. These two points are read from the values of (10) in $y=2^h,2^{h-1}$ and the assignments of ($y=2^j$).

If $t<N^h$, only the values of $1\le y<2^{h-1}$ are protected. If $t=N^h$, an additional protection is provided for $y=2^{h-1}$. For these $y$, their old values are equal to the untruncated $P(y)$. Starting from the 1 term, the steps for the even-left parameter $r=y-1>0$ are given by the addition prefix in the 1 section, projected onto the $A_{t+1}$.

At each required odd step put $r=y-1$, $m=\nu_2(r+1)$ and $w=N^m$, and choose $D=P(r-(2^m-1))$ and $A=P(r)$ as in Section 4. We retain the following bounds.

$$
 r<2^{h-1},\quad m\le h-1,\quad Nw\le t,\quad
 0<A<P(r+1)<2^t.
$$

The final odd step can have $m=h-1$ only at $t=N^h$. Then $D=0$ and $P(r+1)=2^t-2^w<2^t$. This is why at the intermediate orders we stop the induction one entry before the midpoint.

At old order $t$ and new order $t+1$, the local proof in Sections 4–5 uses the same multiplication and period formulas. We verify its three hypotheses on the old row.

1. $A=g*_t r$, therefore it represents $J_g(J_r)$. The endpoint $A*_t a=P(r+1)$ directly comes from the recursion in the old row $g*_t(r+1)=(g*_t r)*_t a$. The remaining prefixes are determined by the second-level expansion of $A_{Nw}$ and the first periodic incrementality, such as (4).
2. Replace the two outer auxiliary indices $N-2,N-1$ with $h-2,h-1$. Since there are at least two 1 in the low $h$ position of the odd $r>1$, we have $f_r(h-2)\ge h$; $r=1$ uses $h\ge4$ and $f_1(2)=4$. Thus

   $$
   f_A(f_g(h-2))\ge t,\qquad f_A(f_g(h-1))>t .
   $$

The previous formula uses the co-variance of the old representatives and $f_g(h)\ge N^h\ge t$. The old representative $Z_g$ is the value of $J_A\circ J_g$ in $A_t$, hence $f_{Z_g}(h-2)\ge t$, and thus $f_{Z_g}(h-1)>t$. For $i<g$, the appendix E is given to $f_i(N)\ge T>N^{h-1}=f_g(h-1)$. The two actual composites in the image of index $N,h-1$ are successively $f_A(f_i(N))>f_A(f_g(h-1))>t$. The truncation is given as $f_{Z_i}(N)\ge t$. If we take equality, using the 3 section to transport from the $i+1$ term to the $g+1$ term will force $f_{Z_g}(h-1)=t$. Therefore, when $i<g$, it is strictly uniform. When $i=g$, $N>h-1$ is sufficient.
3. In the 5 section, $Nw\le t$ ensures that the lower projections and first periodic convergence are all valid, including the boundaries that involve $Nw=t$. (2), (3) hold for any $1\le m\le h-1$.) Therefore, from the two old representations of $Z_i$, we obtain $f_W(Nw)>t$, and subsequently $2\le v\le e_w$ leads to $f_{C_v}(w)>t$. The lemma 6.7 gives $P(r)*_{t+1}a=P(r+1)$. The higher-order background of this step can take any of the aforementioned $r$ as $D$, regardless of the numerical value of $r$.

This proves that the entire specified prefix retains its old values in $A_{t+1}$. The rest of the half-row follows from the ordinary table rules.

* If $t<N^h$, then from $f_g(h)\ge N^h\ge t+1$, it can be known that the period remains $2^h$. In the $A_{t+1}$ with this period, the $2^{h-1}$ term is $2^{t+1}-2^{N^{h-1}}$. Therefore, $f_k(1)<K_m\le f_k(m-1)$. Along the corresponding width chain starting from 1 within A.2, crossing $K_m$, the obtained $k(t_{\alpha\beta})$ satisfies (D1), hence $f_k(m)=f_{k(t_{\alpha\beta})}(f_k(\beta))\ge H$. This conclusion is referred to as a dense terminal estimate.

Also suppose $m\mid s,\ m\le s<K_m$ and

$$
 [k]_{s+m}=2^s x+2^s-1,\qquad 1\le x\le 2^m-2.
$$

Then $f_k(m)\ge H$. We make a downward induction for $x$. If $x=2^m-2$, the first two images are $s$ and at least $s+m$, by the linear bound $f_k(m-1)\ge s+(m-1)m\ge K_m$. Starting from 0 and crossing $K_m$, its left-end image is at least $s\ge m$, so (D1) applies. If $x<2^m-2$, use Dougherty's theorem 7.2 to prove that the displacement is exactly $s$ for the Claim 1. It gives $s<f_k(\alpha)<s+m\le f_k(\beta)$, and the low $s+m$ position of $F=k(t_{\alpha\beta})$ is $2^s x'$, where $x<x'\le 2^m-2$.$F'=F+2^s-1$ satisfies the inductive hypothesis. Because $\operatorname{cr}F>\gamma_s$, and $m\mid s$, the corollary 6.4 gives

$$
 f_k(m)=f_F(f_k(\beta))\ge f_F(s+m)\ge f_{F'}(m)\ge H .
$$

**D.2. A partially filled block of width 16.** Throughout this part, $m=n=16,\ K_m=N,\ H=d$. Let $\mathcal B$ be the condition that $[n,N)$ has at least $n$ occurrences of 1 and at least one 0. Let $\mathcal D(h)$ assert that every $k$ satisfying $\mathcal B$ and $1\le[k]_n\le L$ has $f_k(n)\ge h$. Dougherty's Theorem 7.2(b) with width $n$ and shift 0 directly gives $\mathcal D(N)$. This holds in $A_N$ for every $1\le[k]_n\le L$.

Let's first deal with the high critical points. If $[e]_n=0$, and $e$ satisfies $\mathcal B$, then $f_e(N)\ge d$. In fact, in the $A_N$ row at the $e$ position, $e*r=e+r\ (1\le r\le L)$, because the lower $n$ positions in the middle row are $1,\ldots,L-1$, whose period divides $2^n$, and $e+1\equiv1\bmod 2^n$. Let $F=e(J_L)$. The newly calculated lower $N$ positions are sufficient to identify $[F]_n=L$, and $F$ has the same position as $e$ in $[n,N)$. Its second zero position $b=f_F(1)$ falls at $[n,N)$, so the dense terminal estimate of D.1 gives $f_F(n)\ge d$. Furthermore, $e$ fixes $\gamma_1$, and $f_L(1)=n,\ f_L(n)=N$ comes from theorem 7.2(c), so $f_e(n)=b$ and $f_e(N)=f_F(b)\ge d$. In $A_N$, only these lower positions are read.

Fix the $N<h\le d$, assume the $\mathcal D(h-1)$. If the $k$ satisfies the positional conditions in the $\mathcal D(h)$ and the critical point is the 2 or the 3, write it as $[k]_h=A+i,\ 2^n\mid A,\ 1\le i\le L$, and take the $e=J_A$. In the $A_{h-1}$, use the inductive hypothesis to obtain the $e*r=A+r\ (1\le r\le 2^n-1)$ term by term, because each intermediate row $A+j,\ 1\le j\le L$ satisfies the $\mathcal B$, and the period is a divisor of the $2^n$. Thus, we have the $k\equiv_{\gamma_{h-1}}e(J_i)$. The $J_i$ has the same critical point as the $k$, and the B.3 gives the $f_i(4)\ge N$. The $e$ fixes the $\gamma_4$, and there is the $f_e(N)\ge d$ from the previous section. Therefore

$$
 f_{e(J_i)}(4)\ge d,\quad f_k(4)\ge h-1,\quad
 f_k(n)\ge h-1+(16-4)=h+11.                                         \tag{D3}
$$

The first step estimates an actual embedding. The second transfers the threshold only at the old order.

Now fix $h$, and induce from $i=[k]_n$ down to $L$. At $i=L$, the second zero position is $b<N$, estimated using dense endpoints. Let $1\le i<L$, take the wide 16 across of A.2, and denote it as $F=k(t_{\alpha\beta}),\ i'=[F]_n,\ b'=f_k(\beta)\ge n$. At $F$, all the high 1 positions are inherited, with $i<i'\le L$ and $i'$ being even. Each case is as follows.

* If the upper block of $F$ is full and $i'<L,\ i'\ne M$, then $i'\le65532$. Applying B.3 gives $f_F(n)>T$.

* If it is filled and $i'=L$, then the critical point of $F$ is 1, and the first image is $b'\ge N$. The original bridge on the left end satisfies $f_k(\alpha)=1$. Its critical points can only be 2, 3, 4, and the critical point of 4 will also fill the high blocks. The remaining 2 and 3 are precisely solved by (D3)).

This gives $\mathcal D(h-1)\Rightarrow\mathcal D(h)$, so $\mathcal D(d)$ holds. The terminal estimates and the high critical point estimates used in (D3)) have been proven before these two inductions.

## Appendix E. Periods and additive prefixes of width 256

Let $1\le[k]_N<2^N-2=a$. Prove that $f_k(N)\ge T$, and when $[N,T]$ contains a 1, then $f_k(N)>T$. Take a wide $N$ that spans A.2, and denote it as $H=k(t_{\alpha\beta}),\ r=f_k(\alpha),\ b'=f_k(\beta)$. Then $1\le r<N,\ b'\ge N,\ f_k(N)=f_H(b')$, and $\operatorname{cr}H=\gamma_r,\ f_H(r)=b'$.

* When $4\le r<N$, B.2 and (C2) give $f_H(N)>T$.
* When $r=2,3$, $c=f_H(4)\ge b'+4-r>N$. The first two images of $p=H(C)$ are $1,c$, and $f_H(N)=f_p(c)\ge c^c>T$.
* When $r=1$, bridges $(0,1)$ or $(1,16)$ can only be used. In the former case, the first two images of $k$ are $1,b'$, and A2) provides the required weak bound. If it equals $T$, then $b'=N,\ [k]_d=g$, and there is no compatibility with $[N,T]$ that contains 1.
* For $(1,16)$, $k$ is fixed with $\gamma_1$, and let $c=f_k(4)\ge4,\ p=k(C)$. The first two images of $p$ are $1,c$, and $f_k(N)=f_p(c)$. When $c>N$, it is strict. When $c=N$, there is a weak bound; if it is taken to be equal, then $[p]_d=g$. $p$ is odd, so subtracting 1 retains all digits above the 1 place. From $\operatorname{bits}(k)\subseteq\operatorname{bits}(p-1)$, if $k$ has 1 in $[N,T]$, it leads to a contradiction.
* In the last case where $4\le c<N$ occurs, the small growth boundary of B.3 excludes the critical points of $k$, which are 2 or 3. Therefore, $k$ is fixed at $\gamma_2$, and $f_p(2)=f_k(16)=b'$. The critical point of $P'=p(J_2)$ is $c$, and the first image is $b'$. B.2 and (C2) give $f_{P'}(N)>T$, hence

  $$
   f_k(N)=f_p(c)\ge f_p(4)=f_{P'}(b')\ge f_{P'}(N)>T .
  $$

Therefore, for $0<x<2^d,\ 2^N\mid x$, every $x+i,\ 1\le i<a$ has $1\le[x+i]_N<a$, and there is a 1 in $[N,T]$. Its period is divisible by $A_d$ and $2^N$. Starting from $x*_d1=x+1$, each step can replace $x+1$ with 1, resulting in

$$
 x*_d y=x+y\qquad(1\le y\le a).
$$

This also proves that all the lower-order estimates cited in section 1 can be projected down step by step. The proof only uses the point-like bounds from A to C.

## Appendix F. Eight starting values for the order-by-order proof

All products here are in $A_{h_0}$, with $h_0=N^3+1$. Put

$$
 Q=N^2,\quad Z=2^Q,\quad V=Z-2^N,\quad P_3=2^{N^3},\quad K_0=P_3-Z,
 \qquad a=2^N-2,\quad g=a-1 .
$$

First, calculate $a*a=V$. In fact, $a*y=h_N(y)\ (1\le y\le a)$. The initial value $y=1$ comes from the row successor. For even steps, use (D2), where $m=N,\ H=h_0$. For odd steps, take $3\le y<a$, and $x=h_N(y-1)$. It is a multiple of $2^N$, and $[N,N^2)$ contains at least $N$ 1 and at least one 0. The appendix E is given to $x*a=x+a$. The next step will use the row $x+a$, with the lowest $N$ being $a$, and the second zero falling at $[N,N^2)$. The dense terminal estimate of D.1 is given to $f_{x+a}(N)\ge h_0$, so at this step, the right parameter $x+1$ can be replaced with 1. Thus, $x*(a+1)=x+a+1=h_N(y)$. This completes the prefix induction, especially for $a*a=h_N(a)=V$.

Start from $g*1=a$ and apply $g*(i+1)=(g*i)*a$ at every step. This yields successively

$$
 \begin{array}{c|c@{\qquad}c|c}
 i&g*i&i&g*i\\ \hline
 1&a&5&K_0+a\\
 2&V&6&P_3-2^N\\
 3&Z-2&7&P_3-2\\
 4&K_0&8&P_3 .
 \end{array}                                                       \tag{F1}
$$

Specifically, $V*a=Z-2$ is derived from the appendix E, and $(Z-2)*a=h_Q(a)=K_0$ is derived from the second-level bit stretching $h_Q:A_{N+1}\to A_{Q(N+1)}$ and projection. Taking $J_{K_0}$ as the background, using the displacement $Q$ and width 1 of Dougherty's lemma 6.3, with the protection point being $J_{P_3-1}(\gamma_1)=\gamma_{N^3+1}$, the lowest two zeros are read. Therefore, $K_0*y=K_0+y$ holds true for $1\le y\le Z$, giving the fifth term.

For the sixth item, the low $Q$ digits of $K_0+a$ are $a$, within $1,\ldots,2^Q-2$. The theorem 7.2(b) takes a width of $Q$ and a displacement of 0 to $f_{K_0+a}(Q)\ge Q^2=N^4\ge h_0$, so the period of this row is divisible by $Z$. Because of $Z\mid K_0$,

$$
 (K_0+a)*a=(K_0+a)*(K_0+a)=(K_0*a)*(K_0*a)
          =K_0*(a*a)=K_0+V=P_3-2^N .
$$

The seventh item is again denoted by the appendix E, and the eighth item is $(P_3-2)*a=h_{N^3}(a)\bmod 2^{h_0}=P_3$. The source order used in both cases of expansion is $N+1=257$.
